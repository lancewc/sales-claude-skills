## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: THE CARDINAL RULE OF NEGOTIATION
In enterprise procurement, buyers are trained to demand discounts at the one-yard line:
> *"We want to go with [Your Product], but our budget committee requires an across-the-board 20% discount before we can execute the agreement."*

Amateur sales reps immediately fold. They say: *"Let me ask my VP if we can discount."* By discounting unilaterally, the rep sends two destructive signals:
1. **Our software was overpriced to begin with.**
2. **We are weak negotiators who can be pushed around on terms.**

The cardinal rule of commercial negotiation is: **Never give a concession without getting a concession in return (The Give-to-Get Principle).**

---

## CHAPTER 1: WHERE THE GIVE-TO-GET PRINCIPLE COMES FROM
"Never give a concession without getting one in return" is not a Sales MBA invention. It is a plain-language restatement of a negotiation-theory concept called **logrolling** (reciprocal concession trading): moving on an issue that costs you little in exchange for movement on an issue that matters to you, so both sides walk away having "won" on the dimension they actually cared about.

It sits, with some real tension, between two negotiation traditions worth knowing:
- **Positional / hard-bargaining tradition** (the lineage give-to-get comes from): treat every concession as a trade, extract something of equal or greater value for every inch you give, and never signal that your price was arbitrary.
- **Principled negotiation** (Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton, *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, Harvard Program on Negotiation): cautions against pure positional trading and argues instead for separating the people from the problem, focusing on underlying interests rather than stated positions, and anchoring every trade to **objective, defensible criteria** — market data, cost structure, documented value — rather than to whoever blinks first.
- **Chris Voss** (*Never Split the Difference*, a former FBI lead hostage negotiator) goes further, arguing that **tactical empathy — labeling the other side's position and mirroring their language to understand it — routinely beats naked concession trading**, and that negotiators who jump straight to "I'll do X if you do Y" without first understanding *why* the other side is asking skip the highest-leverage move available.

The synthesis that works in enterprise SaaS negotiation: **use tactical empathy to understand what the buyer actually needs before you trade, then use logrolling — anchored to the objective value case you already built (see Volume 14) — to make sure every concession is reciprocal.** Discounting without a trade is not "principled negotiation"; Fisher and Ury never argued for giving things away. It is capitulation with a hard-bargaining hangover — the buyer knows exactly how it happened, and will ask for the same thing again at renewal.

## CHAPTER 3: THE VERBATIM GIVING-TO-GET TALK-TRACK
When procurement demands a 15% discount:
> *"I understand your procurement guidelines, [Name]. We don't discount our software arbitrarily because our pricing directly funds our dedicated customer success team and SOC2 security infrastructure.
> However, our finance team is willing to trade commercial value. 
> If your team can commit to a **2-year agreement**, pay **annually upfront via ACH**, and agree to participate in a **joint space-optimization case study** once you hit your space reduction milestone, I can justify that 15% adjustment to our CFO.
> Can we agree to move forward on those terms?"*

If the customer refuses your "Get," **you do not give the discount**. Standing firm on value establishes commercial stature that carries through the entire multi-year enterprise relationship.

## CHAPTER 4: SECOND FIELD EXAMPLE — TRADING ON PAYMENT TERMS, NOT PRICE
*Context: Procurement lead David is negotiating final terms for a $60,000 ACV agreement with AE [Your Name].*

> **David (Procurement):** "We're fine on price. Where we need flexibility is payment terms — our AP cycle runs on Net-90, and your standard terms are Net-30. Can you move to Net-90?"
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** *(Tactical empathy / labeling first)* "It sounds like the real constraint here isn't the contract value at all — it's your internal AP calendar. Is that right?"
>
> **David (Procurement):** "Exactly. It's not a budget issue, it's a cash-flow-timing issue on our end."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "That's helpful to know, because it changes what we're actually negotiating. Net-90 on a monthly-invoiced contract creates real collections risk on our side, so I can't do that as a flat give. But if your constraint is timing, not total spend, here's a trade that solves your actual problem: we move to **annual upfront billing** instead of monthly, which eliminates the Net-90 exposure entirely, because there's only one invoice a year instead of twelve. In exchange for the cash-flow certainty that gives us, I can get you a **6% reduction off list** and lock this year's pricing for a second year.
> That solves your AP timing problem, solves my collections risk, and saves you money on top of it. Does annual upfront work on your end, or is there a reason it has to stay monthly?"
>
> **David (Procurement):** "Annual upfront is actually easier for us than monthly Net-90 — one PO instead of twelve. Let's do that."

**The Lesson:** [Your Name] never traded on the issue David originally named (payment terms) in isolation. By labeling the underlying interest first — "a cash-flow-timing issue, not a budget issue" — the AE found a trade (annual upfront billing) that removed the buyer's actual problem, reduced the seller's actual risk, and only then layered a price concession on top, instead of leading with one.

## CHAPTER 5: WHERE REPS GET THIS WRONG
1. **Giving before getting.** Sequence matters. Say the trade out loud as a single conditional sentence ("if you can do X, I can do Y") and get verbal agreement on the buyer's half before you confirm yours. Reversing the order — discounting first and "hoping" for the multi-year commitment later — forfeits all your leverage.
2. **Trading on the wrong lever.** Reflexively offering a price discount when the buyer's actual constraint is payment timing, contract length, or scope wastes your best chip on a problem the buyer doesn't have. Ask what's actually driving the ask before naming a trade (see Chapter 4).
3. **Skipping tactical empathy and going straight to horse-trading.** Voss's core critique of pure logrolling applies directly here: a rep who opens with "I can do 10% off for a 2-year term" before understanding *why* the buyer is asking for a discount at all looks transactional and invites a counter-counter. Label the interest first.
4. **Treating every concession as equally costly.** Not all gives cost you the same. A joint case study or a reference call costs you almost nothing and may be worth a great deal to your marketing team — trade the cheap-to-you, valuable-to-them items first, before ever touching price. This is classic logrolling.
5. **Negotiating without an anchor.** Fisher and Ury's "objective criteria" principle matters here: a discount granted with no reference point (no ROI number, no comparable deal size, no unit economics) looks arbitrary and invites indefinite further pushing. Every trade should be visibly anchored back to the value case, not just to "what the VP will approve."
6. **Caving on the final ask "to just get it signed."** The last-minute concession at the one-yard line is the most expensive one in the entire deal, because it sets the buyer's expectation for the renewal negotiation a year later. If you give at the finish line, get something at the finish line too — even something symbolic, like signature timing or a reference commitment.
