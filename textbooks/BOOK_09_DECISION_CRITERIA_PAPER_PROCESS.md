## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: HOW DEALS ARE WON BEFORE PROPOSALS ARE SENT
In competitive enterprise sales, if you are responding to an RFP (Request for Proposal) that you did not help write, **you have already lost**. The vendor that helped write the RFP has strategically set "trap-door" decision criteria that highlight their strengths and expose your fatal flaws.

Furthermore, once a customer verbally selects you, you enter the deadliest phase of enterprise sales: **The Paper Process**. More deals slip or die during legal, InfoSec, and procurement reviews than during product evaluation.

This volume provides the blueprint for ghost-writing the customer's **Decision Criteria** and orchestrating the **Paper Process** to close on schedule.

---

## A NOTE ON THE SOURCE: TWO LETTERS, ONE DISCIPLINE

Decision Criteria and Paper Process are two separate letters in MEDDPICC — the "DC" and the second "P" — and John McMahon treats them as two different disciplines that both get neglected for the same reason: neither is a technical objection, so they don't feel urgent until they suddenly are. The industry data on both problems is stark. On the Decision Criteria side, published win-rate research shows that RFP respondents who had no hand in shaping the requirements win only a small single-digit percentage of the time, while vendors who helped influence the criteria before the RFP was issued can win 50% or more of the time — the gap this volume's "trap-door" technique is built to close. On the Paper Process side, a SOC 2 Type II report alone typically takes 6–12 months to produce from a cold start, which is exactly why Chapter 2's Week 2 questions exist: discovering in week 10 that a customer's InfoSec team requires a report you do not have is a self-inflicted, and avoidable, quarter-killer.

## CHAPTER 1: THE THREE TIERS OF DECISION CRITERIA

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        THE 3 CRITERIA TIERS                            │
├──────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. TECHNICAL CRITERIA            │ 2. BUSINESS CRITERIA                │
│ • SSO/SAML, SCIM provisioning   │ • Payback period, EBITDA impact     │
│ • Mobile native apps, CAD ingest │ • Implementation time (<3 weeks)    │
├──────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. TRAP-SETTING (SNOWFLAKE) CRITERIA                                   │
│ • Proprietary capabilities unique to [Your Product] that competitors lack   │
│ • Dynamic, real-time spatial CAD re-rendering without third-party fees│
│ • Integrated sensor-less occupancy algorithms                          │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### How to Set a Trap-Door Criterion:
During discovery, identify a unique capability of [Your Product] that competitors cannot easily match, and tie it to severe financial risk:
> *"Sarah, when you evaluate vendors, make sure you require that floor plan changes can be re-rendered in real-time by your facilities team directly. Some legacy platforms charge $1,500 per floor plan change and require a 3-week CAD outsourcing cycle. If you restructure seating quarterly across 5 floors, that’s $30,000 in hidden fees and a month of delay every single time."*

## CHAPTER 2: CONTROLLING THE PAPER PROCESS
The Paper Process encompasses every administrative step required to turn a verbal agreement into cash in the bank:

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        THE PAPER PROCESS PIPELINE                      │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Mutual NDA (Non-Disclosure Agreement)                               │
│ 2. Vendor Security & InfoSec Questionnaire (SOC2 Type II, ISO 27001)   │
│ 3. Data Processing Agreement (DPA & GDPR compliance)                   │
│ 4. Master Services Agreement (MSA) Legal Redlines                      │
│ 5. Commercial Order Form & Statement of Work (SOW)                     │
│ 6. Procurement & Vendor Onboarding (Coupa, Ariba, Zip)                 │
│ 7. Authorized Signature Authority (DocuSign execution)                 │
│ 8. Purchase Order (PO) Issuance by Accounts Payable                    │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The 4 Crucial Paper Process Questions to Ask in Week 2:
1. *"Who has legal signature authority for software commitments above $30,000?"*
2. *"Will your corporate counsel require us to use your standard vendor agreement, or can we redline on our standard SaaS terms?"*
3. *"Does Accounts Payable require an issued Purchase Order (PO) before cutting payment?"*
4. *"What is the standard procurement onboarding timeline in Coupa/Ariba for new technology vendors?"*

## CHAPTER 3: WHERE REPS GET THIS WRONG

### Mistake 1: Waiting Until the Verbal Yes to Ask Paper Process Questions
The single most common cause of quarter-end slippage is a rep who never asks about legal, security, or procurement until the champion says "we want to move forward." By then, a 6–12 month SOC 2 audit gap, a required DPA, or a mandatory Coupa onboarding cycle becomes a surprise instead of a tracked workstream. Ask the Week 2 questions in Chapter 2 during technical evaluation, not after it.

### Mistake 2: Treating Security Review as "IT's Problem"
Once InfoSec has the questionnaire, reps often go quiet and wait to be told the outcome. Elite reps stay personally engaged — checking in on where the review sits, offering to get their own security team on a call to answer follow-ups directly, and tracking the SOC 2/InfoSec item as its own line on the Mutual Action Plan (see Volume 15), not as a black box owned entirely by the customer.

### Mistake 3: Not Having Your Own Paperwork Ready
Reps are frequently surprised that a SOC 2 Type II report takes 6–12 months to produce, but the real failure is not knowing your own company's audit status before the deal reaches this stage. Know in advance whether you can hand over a current report, whether you need a bridge letter, or whether you need to contractually commit to delivering a report within 12 months of signing — a structure most enterprise procurement teams will accept if you propose it proactively rather than scrambling to explain a gap reactively.

### Mistake 4: Sending Your Most Flexible Legal Terms First
Reps under pressure to "just get the redlines moving" sometimes concede the most negotiable terms (liability caps, indemnification scope, auto-renewal) in the very first draft, leaving nothing to trade later when procurement — not legal — comes back for a final round of concessions on price or terms. Hold your standard position on Round 1 and negotiate in Round 2, when you actually know what the customer's real sticking points are.

### Mistake 5: Showing Up to a Wired RFP
If a formal RFP lands with narrow, oddly specific technical requirements you never influenced, you are very likely reading a document a competitor helped write. A vague scope paired with an exhaustive, hyper-detailed questionnaire is the classic signature of a process wired for an incumbent. The fix is not writing a better response — it is doing the Chapter 1 trap-setting work with your own champion *before* any RFP is issued, so the document that eventually goes out reflects your strengths instead of a competitor's.

## CHAPTER 4: GONG TRANSCRIPT: DIAGNOSING A STALLED PAPER PROCESS
*Context: AE [Your Name] speaking with Diane, VP Operations and verified Champion at a mid-size logistics company, in week 9 of what was forecasted to close in week 6. The deal has gone verbally "yes" but has not closed.*

> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Diane, we said this would sign by the end of last month, and I want to be straight with you — from my side, I don't actually know where it's stuck. Can we walk through exactly what's happened since you sent it to legal?"
>
> **Diane (VP Operations):** "Honestly, I'm not totally sure either. I sent your MSA over and I think our counsel had some comments."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Do you know if those comments have come back to our side yet? My AE ops team hasn't seen a redline from your legal team."
>
> **Diane (VP Operations):** "I... don't think so. I think it might still be sitting with our InfoSec team actually, not legal. They mentioned something about a security questionnaire."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "That's really useful to know, and it tells me exactly where to focus. Here's what I'd like to do: can you connect me directly with whoever owns that questionnaire on your InfoSec team? I can get our security lead on a 15-minute call with them this week to answer anything technical, rather than it sitting in an inbox. And separately, can you check with your legal team whether they've actually opened our MSA yet, or whether it's queued behind other work?"
>
> **Diane (VP Operations):** "That makes sense. I'll email both of them today and loop you in directly instead of relaying through me."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Perfect — and Diane, going forward on this one, let's track it like we tracked the technical evaluation: a shared checklist with owners and dates for InfoSec, legal, and procurement, so neither of us is guessing where it sits again."

**Analysis:** The deal did not stall because of a hard objection — it stalled because nobody, including the Champion, actually owned visibility into where it sat inside two parallel review tracks (InfoSec and legal) that were never separately identified or tracked. This is precisely why Chapter 2's Week 2 questions ask about *each* stage of the Paper Process individually rather than asking a single vague "are we good on paperwork?" question. A rep who had asked those four questions in week 2 would have already known InfoSec review existed as its own track and built it into the Mutual Action Plan before it became an invisible bottleneck in week 9.
