## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: THE "CHECK-THE-BOX" COMMODITIZATION TRAP
When buyers evaluate enterprise software, they often issue a list of technical feature demands:
- *"Do you have a mobile app?"*
- *"Can we color-code desks by department?"*
- *"Do you integrate with our turnstiles?"*
- *"Can employees book parking spots?"*

Inexperienced sales reps fall into the **Feature-Checking Trap**. They enthusiastically answer: *"Yes! Yes, we have that! Let me show you!"*

When you answer feature questions directly, you allow the buyer to treat your software as a commodity. They compare your checklist to three competitors, conclude that all tools look similar, and make their final decision based on **who will discount their price the most**.

Top 1% Account Executives never answer a feature request with a simple yes. They use the **3-Step Reframing Protocol** to elevate the feature into a quantifiable business outcome.

## METHODOLOGY GROUNDING: FEATURES, OUTCOMES, AND WHERE THIS PROTOCOL COMES FROM

Keenan's own position on feature questions is direct: buyers don't buy features, they buy the outcome of closing their Gap. His guidance for handling a checklist question in discovery is close to what this volume calls the 3-Step Reframe — don't just say yes and move on; make the prospect explain *why* the capability matters to the problem they're actually trying to solve, and tie it back to their Future State. He also warns against feature-dumping in a demo: rather than surfacing everything the product can do, an AE should hold the demo to a small handful of capabilities — roughly six or fewer — that map directly to confirmed problems. Answering a checklist question with a bare "yes" hands the buyer exactly what they need to commoditize you against two other vendors who can also say yes.

This volume also deliberately borrows from a second, distinct methodology: Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson's ***The Challenger Sale*** (2011), built on research conducted at CEB (now part of Gartner). Their concept of **Commercial Teaching** centers on **the Reframe** — introducing the customer to a problem or a cost they hadn't yet diagnosed, so the moment lands as "I never thought about it that way." It's worth being precise about the difference between the two frameworks: Challenger's reframe is *proactive* — the rep leads with an unprompted insight the customer didn't ask for, often before the customer has even named a problem. The reframe taught in this volume is *reactive* — it starts from a feature request the *customer* already raised, and walks it backward to the business problem underneath it. The two are compatible (a rep can absolutely use a Challenger-style proactive reframe to introduce a problem behind a feature the customer hasn't even thought to ask about yet), but they are not the same move. Conflating them will make your reframe sound like a canned trick instead of genuine curiosity — which defeats the entire purpose.

## CHAPTER 1: THE 3-STEP REFRAMING PROTOCOL

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                     THE 3-STEP REFRAME PROTOCOL                        │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ STEP 1: VALIDATE & CONFIRM                                             │
│ • Confirm capability calmly without sounding desperate.                │
│ • "Yes, our platform supports that natively."                          │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ STEP 2: PROBE THE UNDERLYING MOTIVATION                                │
│ • Discover why that specific feature was placed on their checklist.   │
│ • "Out of curiosity, what specific breakdown prompted that request?"   │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ STEP 3: ELEVATE TO FINANCIAL / STRATEGIC METRICS                       │
│ • Reframe the feature as a mechanism for revenue or margin expansion. │
│ • "How are you currently measuring the financial impact of that leak?" │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## CHAPTER 2: VERBATIM FEATURE REFRAMING PLAYBOOK

### Case 1: The Mobile App Request
- **Prospect:** *"Does [Your Product] have an iOS and Android mobile app?"*
- **Rookie Rep:** *"Yes! It's rated 4.9 stars on the App Store! Let me show you screenshots!"*
- **Elite AE Reframe:**
  > *"Yes, we support both iOS and native Android apps with single-sign-on. But tell me: when your team evaluated the mobile experience, what specific friction were employees encountering in your current setup that made mobile an essential requirement?"*
- **Prospect Reveal:** *"Employees tell us that if they have to log onto a desktop laptop to book a desk, they just won't do it. They show up unannounced and cause seating chaos."*
- **AE Elevation:** *"So mobile isn't about convenience—it's about driving 95%+ daily compliance so your spatial utilization data is clean enough for the CFO to make multi-million dollar lease consolidation decisions. Is that right?"*

### Case 2: The Badge Scanner Integration Request
- **Prospect:** *"Do you integrate with Lenel/HID badge turnstiles?"*
- **Elite AE Reframe:**
  > *"We integrate with all major access control systems including Lenel, HID, and Software House. But walk me through what you hope to achieve with that badge telemetry: are you trying to track employee attendance for HR compliance, or are you trying to measure the variance between lobby entries and active floor occupancy to downsize real estate?"*

### Case 3: The Analytics Dashboard Request
- **Prospect:** *"Does [Your Product] have a reporting dashboard showing space utilization trends over time?"*
- **Rookie Rep:** *"Yes! You get a full dashboard with over 40 different charts and export options!"*
- **Elite AE Reframe:**
  > *"We do — heat maps, day-of-week trends, department-level utilization, all exportable. But tell me: who inside your organization is asking for that data today, and what decision are they currently unable to make without it?"*
- **Prospect Reveal:** *"Our CFO keeps asking Facilities to justify why we haven't subleased the west wing, and honestly, we don't have real data to defend keeping it — or giving it up."*
- **AE Elevation:** *"So the dashboard isn't a reporting nice-to-have — it's the evidence your CFO needs to make a defensible sublease decision at your next lease event. If you walked into that conversation with three months of hard utilization data instead of a guess, what would that be worth to you?"*

## CHAPTER 4: WHERE REPS GET THIS WRONG

The 3-Step Reframing Protocol is simple to describe and easy to abandon under pressure. The most common failure patterns:

1. **Reverting to the Feature-Checking Trap under time pressure.** A rep who feels the call slipping will default to "yes, let me show you" because it feels productive. It isn't — it just accelerates the buyer's path to a commodity comparison.
2. **Interrogating instead of staying curious.** Step 2 only works with a genuinely curious tone ("out of curiosity...", "help me understand..."). Deliver it flatly or skeptically and the buyer hears an accusation, not a question.
3. **Skipping Step 3 and leaving the reframe half-finished.** Validating the feature and uncovering the reason behind it is only two-thirds of the job. Reps who never explicitly reconnect the answer to a dollar figure or strategic outcome leave the buyer's checklist mentality fully intact.
4. **Overpromising in Step 1 to keep the conversation alive.** Claiming "yes" to a capability that's actually a roadmap item, a costly workaround, or a partner integration creates a trust and contractual problem later — validate honestly, even when the honest answer is "not yet, and here's why that might matter less than you think."
5. **Turning the reframe into a scripted deflection.** Buyers can tell when "out of curiosity, why do you ask?" is a canned stall tactic rather than real interest. Vary the language, and actually listen to the answer instead of waiting for your turn to pivot to ROI.
6. **Never closing the loop.** The protocol isn't complete until the buyer explicitly confirms the elevated reframe ("is that right?") — without that confirmation, the new framing is the rep's assertion, not the buyer's own words, and it won't survive being repeated to their economic buyer.
