## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: THE SINGLE-THREADED GRAVEYARD
The "6.8 stakeholders" number quoted in almost every sales deck traces back to CEB's 2016 research behind *The Challenger Customer* — CEB was acquired by Gartner in 2017 — where researcher Brent Adamson found the average B2B buying group had grown from 5.4 stakeholders in late 2014 to 6.8. Gartner's own more recent buying-journey research puts today's typical buying group at **5 to 11 stakeholders spanning roughly 5 distinct business functions**, with complex enterprise purchases regularly touching as many as 20 people in some capacity. Whichever exact figure you quote, the trend line only moves one direction: buying groups have gotten larger, not smaller, every time this has been measured.

Each stakeholder belongs to a different department, operates under a distinct incentive structure, and — in enterprise software specifically — frequently carries de facto veto authority over the project even without a formal title change.

When an Account Executive is "single-threaded"—relying solely on one contact—their deal is sitting in the graveyard. If that contact leaves the company, gets overwhelmed with other projects, or fails to convince the CFO, the opportunity vanishes overnight.

Elite AEs operate as organizational cartographers: they systematically map and engage all **4 Enterprise Personas** in Week 1 of the sales cycle.

### FIELD BENCHMARKS: WHAT THE DATA ACTUALLY SHOWS
- **Multithreading changes win rate, not just deal size.** Gong's analysis of roughly 1.8 million opportunities found that closed-won deals carry, on average, twice as many engaged buyer contacts as closed-lost deals, and that multithreading is associated with a 130% lift in win rate on deals over $50k. Selling teams were also 67% larger on closed-won deals than on closed-lost ones — multithreading is a discipline on both sides of the table, not just something you hope the buyer does.
- **More stakeholders is a double-edged sword.** CEB's original research also found that the *likelihood* of a purchase decision being made at all actually drops once more than five stakeholders enter the conversation. More people means more competing priorities and more ways for a deal to stall in "no decision" — not just more allies to win over. This is exactly why persona mapping in this volume has to be paired with the Mutual Action Plan discipline in Volume 15: multithreading without a shared plan just multiplies the number of people who can independently create delay.

## CHAPTER 2: THE CHAMPION VS. THE FRIENDLY COACH TEST
Reps confuse a **Friendly Coach** (someone who likes you and will answer your questions) with a true **Champion** (someone who will spend their own political capital to get you across the finish line) more often than any other persona-mapping mistake. The distinction matters because it's the difference between a deal with real internal momentum and one where an AE is celebrating "warmth" that has zero authority behind it.

A true Champion must pass three tests:
1. They have personal pain that your product solves — their own scorecard improves if the deal closes.
2. They have real influence and standing access to the Economic Buyer — not just a line on the org chart, an actual working relationship.
3. They give you information a Friendly Coach never will: budget cycles, competing initiatives, who else is being evaluated, and where the political risk actually sits.

### Worked Example: Surfacing a Friendly Coach Before It's Too Late
- **Scenario:** After three strong discovery calls, the Workplace Experience Manager says: *"I really love this. Let me pull our internal notes together and I'll present it to the CFO myself next month."*
- **The trap:** This sounds like Champion behavior — she's volunteering to carry the message. But she hasn't passed Test 2 (standing access to the EB) or Test 3 (information sharing), and "next month" with no owner outside her own calendar is a soft date, not a milestone.
- **Elite Script:**
  > *"Jennifer, I really appreciate that — and I want to set you up to win that conversation, not put you in a position where you're defending numbers you didn't build. CFOs push hard on lease-liability assumptions and payback math that we've already built the model for. Rather than you presenting alone, let's co-present: you own the operational story, I own the financial model and can answer anything she throws at it live. That also means if she has follow-up questions afterward, you're not stuck fielding them by yourself. Does she have 20 minutes the week of the 14th?"*
- **What this accomplishes:** It reframes "let me handle it" into a joint session, which is the fastest way to find out whether Jennifer can actually get 20 minutes on the CFO's calendar. If she can't, you've just discovered — weeks earlier than if you'd handed her a deck and waited — that she's a Friendly Coach, not a Champion.

## CHAPTER 3: DEFEATING THE SHADOW BLOCKER (INFOSEC & LEGAL)
The biggest mistake in enterprise sales is waiting until Stage 4 to engage Information Security and Legal. 

When an AE announces: *"We agreed on price! Now we just need to send it to legal,"* they are walking into a buzzsaw. InfoSec reviews can take 6 weeks, and corporate legal redlines can derail a contract for 30 days unless pre-empted in Stage 2.

### The Week-2 Pre-Emption Script:
> *"Sarah, most enterprise rollouts get delayed 4 to 6 weeks in legal and IT security reviews. While you and I build the commercial business case, let's connect our security architects with your CISO team this Thursday. We will submit our SOC2 Type II, SAML SSO documentation, and standard DPA so your security gates are 100% cleared by the time the CFO approves the budget."*

## CHAPTER 4: COMMON MISTAKES — WHERE REPS GET PERSONA MAPPING WRONG
1. **Treating the org chart as the buying committee.** A VP title tells you nothing about whether someone actually has budget authority, veto power, or genuine influence. Map roles by function — who really has to say yes — before you map titles.
2. **Only mapping personas after a deal stalls.** By the time a deal has gone quiet, the InfoSec review that should have started in Week 2 hasn't started at all. Persona mapping is a Week 1 activity, not a rescue tactic.
3. **Confusing enthusiasm with authority.** A User Influencer who loves the product in a demo has validated usability — nothing more. Their enthusiasm doesn't substitute for Economic Buyer sign-off or a cleared Shadow Blocker review.
4. **Waiting until legal redlines arrive to speak to the Shadow Blocker.** Holding off on InfoSec and Legal until Stage 4 is the single most common cause of a "verbal yes" that quietly dies in procurement.
5. **Assuming one champion is enough at enterprise scale.** At 6-10+ stakeholders, even a strong champion cannot personally carry a deal through every department's review alone. Elite AEs cultivate a secondary champion — often the User Influencer or a second department lead — as insurance against the primary champion leaving the company or simply losing bandwidth mid-cycle.
