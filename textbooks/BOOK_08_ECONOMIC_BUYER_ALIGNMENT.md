## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: THE DEFINITION OF AN ECONOMIC BUYER
In John McMahon’s MEDDPICC methodology, the **Economic Buyer (EB)** is the only person in the customer's organization who possesses three distinct powers:
1. **Discretionary Checkbook Authority:** They can release funds that were not formally budgeted.
2. **Veto Power:** They can unilaterally kill a project even if every department manager loves it.
3. **Reallocation Authority:** They can pull $50,000 from marketing or HR and give it to facilities to fund your software.

If you have never spoken with the Economic Buyer, **your deal is unforecastable**. You are relying on second-hand hearsay from subordinates who rarely understand how executive capital allocation decisions are made.

---

## A NOTE ON THE SOURCE: WHY "ECONOMIC BUYER" IS ITS OWN LETTER

MEDDPICC dedicates an entire letter to the Economic Buyer — separate from Champion, separate from Decision Criteria — because John McMahon's own forecasting history taught him that the three powers above (discretionary spend, veto, and reallocation) are almost never held by the person your Champion reports to informally. They are held by whoever controls the specific budget category your purchase falls into, and in a matrixed enterprise that can be a CFO, a divisional COO, or a VP two levels below the C-suite with a hard dollar ceiling on their signature authority. The methodology's insistence on *direct* contact with this person — not a summary relayed by your Champion — exists because subordinates routinely misreport what actually moves an executive to approve unbudgeted spend. A Champion can tell you what the EB *seems* to care about; only the EB can confirm it.

## CHAPTER 2: HOW TO ACCESS THE EB WITHOUT ALIENATING YOUR CHAMPION
The number one fear of a sales rep is offending their champion: *"If I ask to meet their boss, they will get angry and think I don't trust them."*

Elite AEs never bypass the champion; they **elevate** the champion.

### The 4-Step Access Framework:
1. **Praise their domain expertise:** Acknowledge that the champion knows the operational details better than anyone.
2. **Warn them of executive hazards:** Explain that CFOs grill project sponsors on complex financial metrics (ASC 842 lease capitalization, EBITDA arbitrage) that software quotes don't cover.
3. **Offer joint sponsorship:** Position the meeting as a co-presentation where the champion shines as the strategic innovator.
4. **Draft the invitation:** Write the exact email your champion can forward to the EB.

## CHAPTER 3: THE 20-MINUTE EXECUTIVE BRIEFING BLUEPRINT
When you finally earn 20 minutes with the CFO or COO, **never give a software demo**. Follow this strict executive agenda:

- **Minute 0–3 (Strategic Alignment):** Verify their macro corporate mandate (e.g., *"Dan, Laura mentioned your executive priority this fiscal year is cutting real estate OpEx by 15% while supporting hybrid flexibility. Is that accurate?"*)
- **Minute 4–10 (The Quantified Problem):** Show their current financial bleed using their actual real estate lease metrics.
- **Minute 11–15 (The Business Case & EBITDA Impact):** Present the 1-page financial model showing net EBITDA expansion and payback in under 60 days.
- **Minute 16–20 (Decision Process & Next Steps):** Agree on the commercial criteria required to greenlight the contract: *"Dan, if our 30-day technical validation proves this $450k lease reduction, do you see any barrier to approving this agreement by October 31st?"*

## CHAPTER 4: WHERE REPS GET THIS WRONG

### Mistake 1: Mistaking a Budget Holder for the Economic Buyer
A department head who manages an already-approved line-item budget is not automatically an Economic Buyer. If they can only spend what was pre-allocated to their cost center and cannot pull incremental dollars from elsewhere, they lack Reallocation Authority — one of the three defining powers. Ask directly: *"If this exceeds your current software budget line, who approves the overage?"* If the answer is someone else, you have not yet found the EB.

### Mistake 2: Accepting a "Figurehead" Meeting
Getting an EB into a room is not the same as getting their commitment. Reps often let the Champion do all the talking in the executive briefing and leave without the EB personally confirming the business case or the decision criteria. If the EB says nothing beyond "sounds good, keep me posted," you have not actually aligned with them — you have merely been introduced.

### Mistake 3: Bypassing the Champion Instead of Elevating Them
Going over your Champion's head to reach the EB unannounced — even with good intentions — is read internally as disrespect and frequently damages the one relationship you depend on for internal intelligence. The 4-Step Access Framework in Chapter 2 exists specifically to avoid this failure mode.

### Mistake 4: Treating the EB Meeting as an FYI Instead of a Commitment Extraction
Walking out of a 20-minute EB briefing without a dated next step is a wasted meeting. Every EB conversation should end with an explicit, time-bound commercial or process commitment, as modeled in Minute 16–20 above — never a vague "let's stay in touch."

### Mistake 5: Assuming the Highest Title in the Room Is the EB
In large or matrixed organizations, the most senior person on a call is not always the one with checkbook authority for *this specific category* of spend. A CEO may happily defer facilities software decisions entirely to a COO with a hard budget ceiling. Confirm authority for this purchase specifically — don't infer it from seniority alone.

## CHAPTER 5: GONG TRANSCRIPT: EARNING EB ACCESS THROUGH THE CHAMPION
*Context: AE [Your Name] speaking with Marcus, VP of IT Security at a regional healthcare network, after two strong technical validation calls. Marcus has shown early signs of being a genuine Champion.*

> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Marcus, the technical team is fully bought in, which is great news. Here's what I've seen happen next in deals this size, though: the CISO loves the tool, but when the CFO, Elena, sees a $95,000 line item she's never heard of, she asks the CISO to defend it cold — no context, no ROI model, just a number. That's a hard spot to put you in. I'd rather we get ahead of it."
>
> **Marcus (VP IT Security):** "That's... actually exactly what happened with our last vendor. Elena killed it in one meeting."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Right — and I'd bet that vendor's rep never sat in the room with her either. Let's not repeat that. You know the breach-risk numbers better than anyone on my team; I know how to translate that into the financial model Elena is going to want to see. If we build the one-pager together and you walk her through it with me there for technical backup, you're the one presenting the win — not defending a surprise."
>
> **Marcus (VP IT Security):** "I like that better than me trying to explain your pricing model solo. Let me see if I can get 20 minutes on her calendar next week."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Perfect. One more thing — when you reach out to her team, would it help if I drafted the calendar invite language? I can frame it as a joint briefing on breach-risk exposure reduction, not a vendor pitch, so it lands the right way with her assistant."
>
> **Marcus (VP IT Security):** "Yes, send that over today. I'll forward it as soon as I get it."

**Analysis:** Notice the sequence: [Your Name] never asked Marcus to "introduce me to your CFO" in the abstract. The AE named the specific executive hazard (a cold, unsupported budget line), offered joint sponsorship that lets Marcus shine, and then removed friction by drafting the actual invitation Marcus would otherwise have had to write himself. This is the 4-Step Access Framework executed in real time — and it is why Marcus asked for the draft instead of being asked to produce one from scratch.
