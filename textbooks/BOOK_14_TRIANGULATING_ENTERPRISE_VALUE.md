## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: THE MULTI-DEPARTMENTAL MATRIX
Enterprise workplace software sits at the intersection of four corporate departments: **Corporate Real Estate (CRE/Facilities), Human Resources (People Ops), Information Technology (IT/InfoSec), and Finance (CFO)**.

Each department speaks a completely different language:
- Facilities cares about square footage, lease rates, and CAD drawings.
- HR cares about employee retention, collaboration, and commute sentiment.
- IT cares about SAML SSO, SCIM user provisioning, and SOC2 compliance.
- Finance cares about OpEx reduction, payback periods, and EBITDA expansion.

If an AE delivers the same generic pitch to all four groups, the deal collapses into departmental conflict. An elite AE executes **Stakeholder Triangulation**: delivering customized value propositions tailored specifically to each stakeholder’s core scorecard.

### FIELD BENCHMARKS: WHAT THE DATA ACTUALLY SHOWS
- **A single pitch will not survive four departments.** Buying-group messaging research consistently finds that if you present the identical value proposition to Finance, IT, HR, and Facilities, you lose credibility with most of them — each function evaluates the same purchase against a different scorecard, and generic messaging reads as "sales speak" to the two or three groups it wasn't built for.
- **Tailor the evidence, not the story.** The pitches that hold up under stakeholder scrutiny aren't four unrelated products — they're one consistent business outcome (e.g., "reduce real estate waste without hurting the employee experience") delivered with role-specific proof underneath it: the CFO gets the financial model, IT gets the security and integration spec, HR gets the retention and experience data, Facilities gets the utilization numbers. Keep the throughline consistent even as the evidence changes, or the four departments will compare notes and conclude they were told four different things.

## CHAPTER 2: WORKED EXAMPLE — WHEN TWO DEPARTMENTS PULL IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS
Triangulation gets hard when one department's win condition creates friction for another. The most common version: Facilities wants speed (get the pilot running before the lease renewal deadline) while IT wants rigor (a full security review before any pilot touches their SSO). If an AE tries to referee this without preparation, the deal stalls in a scheduling fight that looks like a technical objection.

- **Scenario:** The Facilities lead says: *"We need this live in three weeks before our lease renewal decision — can IT just fast-track their review?"* The IT Director, hearing that secondhand, tells the Champion: *"We're not skipping our security process for someone else's deadline."*
- **The trap:** Picking a side. Siding with Facilities on timeline burns trust with IT before the SOC2 review even starts; siding with IT on process makes Facilities feel like their deadline doesn't matter.
- **Elite Script (said directly to both, together):**
  > *"Here's what I'm hearing, and I want to name it so we solve it together instead of around each other. Facilities has a hard external deadline — the lease decision on the 30th. IT has a non-negotiable internal process — the security review, which normally runs about two weeks once it starts. Those aren't actually in conflict if we start the security review today, in parallel with the commercial conversation, instead of waiting for a signed order form to kick it off. That gets IT its full two weeks and gets Facilities an answer before the lease deadline. Can we get 30 minutes with your security team this week to kick that off, independent of where we land commercially?"*
- **What this accomplishes:** It reframes a resourcing fight into a sequencing fix, gives each department the thing it actually needs — a deadline respected, a process respected — and pulls the Shadow Blocker persona from Volume 13 into the timeline before it becomes the reason the deal misses its date.

## CHAPTER 3: ORCHESTRATING THE EXECUTIVE CONSENSUS MEETING
Once all four stakeholders have been individual briefed, assemble them for a 30-minute **Executive Consensus Alignment Session**:
1. Review the shared corporate objective (e.g., modernizing the hybrid office while cutting $500k in unnecessary lease liability).
2. Validate that Facilities, HR, and IT have all verified their respective technical and operational gates.
3. Present the unified business case to the CFO for formal signoff.

## CHAPTER 4: COMMON MISTAKES — WHERE REPS GET TRIANGULATION WRONG
1. **Sending the same one-pager to all four departments.** If Finance and IT both receive a PDF titled "ROI and Security Overview," neither reads it as written for them — and the giveaway, when they compare notes, damages trust with all four.
2. **Leading with product features instead of the department's own metric.** Facilities does not care that a platform has "AI-powered heatmaps" — it cares about the lease renewal decision the heatmap data supports. Translate every feature into the receiving department's scorecard before you mention it.
3. **Letting departments hear about each other secondhand.** When Facilities and IT get conflicting summaries of the same deal through internal Slack messages or hallway conversations, the AE loses control of the narrative. Get all four in the same room — or the same thread — before assumptions calcify.
4. **Bringing in Finance last, as a rubber stamp.** Finance is often triangulated at the very end because AEs assume the champion will "handle the budget conversation." Finance should see the OpEx/EBITDA framing as early as Facilities sees the utilization framing, not as a final formality.
5. **Treating the Executive Consensus meeting as a first exposure instead of a ratification.** If the 30-minute alignment session in Chapter 3 is the first time all four stakeholders hear the unified business case, it will surface new objections live, in the room. It should confirm what each stakeholder already agreed to individually.
