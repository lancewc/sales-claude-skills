## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: WHY 90% OF SOFTWARE DEMOS FAIL
The standard corporate software demo is an excruciating linear harbor tour. The sales engineer or AE logs into a demo environment, clicks through login settings, explains user profile preferences, walks through five sub-menus, and spends 35 minutes showing administrative configuration screens.

By Minute 40, the executive buyer is checking emails on their phone, completely disengaged. When the rep finally shows the most valuable analytics screen at Minute 48, the buyer announces: *"Sorry, I have a hard stop. Send me the deck."*

Peter Cohan, author of *Great Demo!*, revolutionized enterprise software demonstrations with a single counter-intuitive principle: **Do The Last Thing First**.

---

## CHAPTER 1: THE INVERTED DEMO PYRAMID
Instead of saving your best screen for the grand finale, **show the ultimate executive payoff screen in Minute 3**.

```
TRADITIONAL LINEAR DEMO (LOSING):
[Login] ──► [Admin Settings] ──► [Workflows] ──► [Menus] ──► [PAYOFF SCREEN AT MIN 45] (Buyer already asleep)

THE INVERTED PYRAMID (WINNING):
[PAYOFF SCREEN AT MINUTE 3] ──► [Show Delta] ──► [Only Show Steps Prospect Demands to See]
```

### The 4-Step Inverted Demonstration Flow:
1. **The Context Recap (Min 0–2):** Summarize the pain, numbers, and goals uncovered in discovery.
2. **The Payoff Screen (Min 2–5):** Immediately open to the finished executive dashboard that solves their primary financial problem.
3. **The Delta Contrast (Min 5–10):** Contrast their current painful manual state with this automated outcome.
4. **The Drill-Down (Min 10–25):** Only peel back the layers and workflows that the buyer specifically asks to see.

## CHAPTER 2: WORKED EXAMPLE — THE MINUTE-3 PAYOFF
*Context: AE [Your Name] presenting [Your Product] to the VP of Real Estate and Chief Operating Officer.*

> **[Your Name] (AE):** *(Sharing screen directly to [Your Product]'s Executive Space Optimization Dashboard)*
> "Laura, Marcus, thank you for joining. In our discovery call with Laura on Tuesday, she shared that your company is currently spending $2.4M annually across 55,000 square feet in Chicago, and your biggest headache is having zero visibility into which departments are actually occupying desks on Tuesdays versus Fridays.
> Rather than walking you through how to configure account settings, I want to show you the end result first.
> What you are looking at right now on my screen is the **Executive Spatial Portfolio Audit**.
> Notice this metric in the top right: **Floor 14 Peak Utilization: 32%**.
> By looking at this single screen, your executive committee can immediately see that by consolidating Marketing and Customer Success into Neighborhood Zone B, you can completely surrender 15,000 square feet of leased space on Floor 14 before your October renewal.
> That single decision removes **$600,000 in lease liability** from your balance sheet this year.
> Marcus, if this was the only report your team had when you walked into your board meeting next month, how would this change your lease renewal strategy?"
>
> **Marcus (COO):** "That would completely change our leverage with the landlord. We wouldn't renew the full floor. But how do you get this data? How hard is it for employees to log their seats?"
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Great question. Let me show you the exact two clicks an employee takes to reserve a neighborhood desk."

### Why This Closed the Deal:
- **Instant Dopamine:** In under 180 seconds, the COO saw a $600,000 solution to his business problem.
- **Pull vs. Push:** The COO asked to see the feature ("How do you get this data?"), transforming the demo from a passive pitch into an active inquiry.

## CHAPTER 3: GROUNDING THE METHOD — WHAT COHAN ACTUALLY WROTE

"Inverted Pyramid" is our own teaching label for a real, well-documented methodology, and it is worth being precise about where the label ends and Peter Cohan's actual terms begin, so you can speak about this credibly with anyone who has read the book or attended a workshop.

**The source.** Peter Cohan is the founder of The Second Derivative and the author of *Great Demo!: How to Create and Execute Stunning Software Demonstrations*, now in its third edition. "Do the Last Thing First" is his own phrase and the spine of the entire book — not a rebranding invented by any single company's enablement team. In interviews he has described it with a simple analogy: a recipe book that opens with a photograph of the finished, plated meal before it makes you read the cooking instructions. You decide whether you want to eat the dish before you decide whether you want to read the recipe.

**What Cohan calls the opposite failure mode.** The demo this textbook calls a "harbor tour" is Cohan's own term too, not an outside metaphor grafted on. He uses it in his own writing to describe a long, undifferentiated walkthrough that tries to cover every possible feature before any real discussion of the specific prospect's situation — the software equivalent of a boat that circles the harbor for three hours while the passenger keeps asking "have you seen anything you like yet?" and can't get off until the tour ends.

**The payoff screen has a name.** What this chapter calls the "Payoff Screen" is what Cohan calls an **Illustration** — a small, purpose-built demonstration of one specific capability, built and sequenced to answer one specific piece of discovery, not a tour of the product. A full Great Demo! is assembled from a handful of Illustrations, each one earned by something the prospect told you in discovery, not by what your product happens to be good at showing off.

**The opening isn't improvised — it's a Situation Slide.** Before Cohan ever opens the software, he opens with what he calls a **Situation Slide**: a one-to-two-minute, plain-language recap of who is in the room, why they're there, what specifically you're going to show, and — critically — the *Delta* (the gap between their current state and the state they want) plus any critical date driving urgency. Step 1 in this chapter's 4-Step flow ("The Context Recap") is our name for exactly this. If you skip it and jump straight to the payoff screen, the buyer sees an impressive dashboard with no anchor for why it should matter to them specifically.

**"Do the Last Thing First" is not the same as "show the flashiest screen first."** This is the single most common misreading of the method. The "last thing" is not your product's most visually impressive screen — it is the *specific, numeric answer to the specific problem the prospect told you about in discovery*. If your discovery only produced generic pain ("we're inefficient"), you do not have enough to build a real payoff screen; you have enough for a **Vision Generation Demo** instead (see Chapter 5).

**Qualification is not discovery, and Cohan is blunt about the difference.** Frameworks like BANT or MEDDIC tell you whether a deal is worth pursuing — budget, authority, need, timeline. They do not tell you what to put on the screen in Minute 3. Cohan's own test for demo-readiness is simpler and harder to fake: *"Do we know what to show — and why?"* A rep who has qualified a deal but never asked "which floor, which lease renewal date, which dollar figure" has nothing to invert. Confirmed discovery numbers are the raw material of the Inverted Pyramid; without them, "Do the Last Thing First" collapses back into a generic, harbor-tour-style walkthrough with one flashy screen moved to the front.

## CHAPTER 4: A SECOND WORKED EXAMPLE — A DIFFERENT VERTICAL, THE SAME INVERSION

The Inverted Pyramid is not a real-estate-specific trick; it is a structural principle that applies to any B2B software category. Here it is again in a completely different vertical, to show the pattern rather than the industry.

*Context: [Your Name] (AE) presenting [Your Product], a customer-support operations platform, to the VP of Customer Support and the CFO of a mid-market SaaS company. Discovery uncovered that the support team is missing its contractual SLA on 22% of enterprise tickets, and the company's largest customer has an at-risk renewal in six weeks tied directly to response-time performance.*

> **[Your Name] (AE):** *(Sharing screen directly to [Your Product]'s Executive SLA Recovery Dashboard)*
> "Priya, Tom, thanks for making time. On our call last week, Priya, you told me that 22% of your enterprise tickets are breaching your 4-hour SLA, and that Meridian Health — your largest account at $840,000 ARR — has an executive review in six weeks where SLA performance is explicitly on the agenda.
> Instead of walking through how tickets get created or how your team configures routing rules, I want to show you the number your board is going to ask about at that review.
> This is what your dashboard shows this morning: **Enterprise SLA compliance, trailing 30 days.**
> Right now, across your whole book, you're at 78%. But look at this second number next to it — this is a live simulation using your actual ticket volume from the last 30 days, run through our auto-routing and escalation rules. Compliance moves to **96%**, and average time-to-first-response on enterprise tickets drops from 5.2 hours to 51 minutes.
> Tom, if Priya walked into that Meridian Health review in six weeks with a 96% compliance number instead of 78%, how does that change that conversation?"
>
> **Tom (CFO):** "That changes it completely — that account is a third of our net revenue retention target for the quarter. How fast could we actually get to that number, realistically?"
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Good — that's exactly the right question. Let me show you the three settings that get you there, and how long each one takes to turn on."

### Why This Works:
- **The payoff is a number the CFO already cares about, not a feature tour.** Compliance percentage and time-to-first-response are the exact metrics discovery surfaced — nothing was invented for the demo.
- **The "last thing" is genuinely the last thing.** 96% compliance and the Meridian Health renewal are the end of this deal's story, shown in the first three minutes instead of buried in a feature walkthrough.
- **The buyer moves from skeptic to project sponsor** by asking "how fast" — an implementation question, not a feature question — because the destination has already been sold.

## CHAPTER 5: WHERE REPS GET THIS WRONG

The Inverted Pyramid is simple to describe and surprisingly easy to execute badly. The most common failure modes:

1. **Skipping discovery and guessing at a payoff screen.** Reps under time pressure sometimes open with an impressive-looking dashboard that was never tied to anything the prospect actually said. The buyer sees a nice screen, not *their* answer, and the "Wow" never lands. If you cannot fill in the Situation Slide with the prospect's own numbers, you are not ready to invert the pyramid — you need a **Vision Generation Demo** (a short, discovery-light demo whose only job is to earn a real discovery conversation), not a full Great Demo!.
2. **Confusing "last thing first" with "flashiest thing first."** Teams sometimes standardize on one "wow screen" and open every demo with it regardless of what discovery uncovered. This is a harbor tour wearing an inverted-pyramid costume — the screen changes, but it is still generic.
3. **Opening the payoff screen with no context recap.** Skipping the Situation Slide and jumping straight to the dashboard disorients the buyer; they see something impressive but have no frame for why it matters to their specific numbers.
4. **Burying the payoff by immediately moving on.** After showing the payoff screen, some reps rush into a full feature walkthrough instead of pausing. Cohan's structure depends on the pause — the temperature-check question ("how would this change your renewal strategy?") is not optional color, it is what converts the payoff from a screen you showed into a decision the buyer is now making out loud.
5. **Treating qualification as if it were discovery.** A rep who knows budget, authority, and a rough timeline (BANT) but never got the specific number, date, or business consequence has qualified the deal but not prepared the demo. The two are different jobs.

### Field Exercise:
Take your last three demos and, for each one, write down the exact discovery fact (a number, a date, or a named business consequence) that your opening screen was built to answer. If you cannot fill in that sentence for any of the three, that demo was a harbor tour with better production values.
