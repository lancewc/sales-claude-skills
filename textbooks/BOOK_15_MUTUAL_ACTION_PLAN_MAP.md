## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: CLOSING IS PROJECT MANAGEMENT
Amateur sales reps view closing as an emotional persuasion event: *"If I use the right high-pressure closing line at the end of the month, they will sign."*

Enterprise Account Executives know that closing is simply **Project Management**. An enterprise purchase requires 20+ distinct tasks executed across multiple legal, technical, and operational teams.

The **Mutual Action Plan (MAP)** — also called a Mutual Close Plan, Joint Execution Plan (JEP), or Mutual Success Plan depending on which CRM or sales methodology vendor coined the term at your company — transforms a one-sided sales chase into a collaborative, mutual project plan agreed upon with the customer.

### FIELD BENCHMARKS: WHAT THE DATA ACTUALLY SHOWS
- **MAPs correlate with materially higher win rates.** Analysis of platform data from Outreach found that deals where AEs engaged the buyer with a mutual action plan closed at a 26% higher win rate than deals without one. The lift tracks with how actively the plan is kept alive, not with the mere existence of a document.
- **A MAP only works if every milestone has a named person, not a department.** The plans that actually move deals assign a real name and a real deadline to every action — buyer side and seller side alike — instead of vague owners like "Procurement" or "Legal."
- **Executives will not read a 40-step plan.** Senior buyer-side stakeholders typically give a shared plan only seconds of attention; a MAP with dozens of equally-weighted steps gets skimmed once and ignored. Effective MAPs surface 6-10 milestones, not a full project-management Gantt chart — which is exactly the scope of the 8-Stage Blueprint in Chapter 3.

## CHAPTER 1: THE RULE OF THE ANCHOR EVENT
A MAP is never anchored to *"When will you sign our contract?"* Buyers do not care about signing your contract. They care about their own internal business milestones.
- **Wrong (Selfish) Anchor:** *"Can we get signatures by September 30th for our fiscal quarter?"*
- **Right (Customer-Centric) Anchor:** *"Sarah, you stated that you must announce the new hybrid seating policy on November 1st before the Chicago lease expires on December 31st. To safely launch on November 1st, we must work backward to establish each required milestone."*

## CHAPTER 2: BUILD IT WITH THEM, NOT FOR THEM
The single biggest reason a Mutual Action Plan gets ignored is that it was never actually mutual — an AE built it alone in a spreadsheet, attached it to a follow-up email, and hoped the buyer would treat it as gospel. A plan the buyer didn't help write is just a vendor checklist wearing a collaborative name, and buyers routinely treat it exactly that way: unopened.

The fix is mechanical, not conceptual: build the first draft live, on a shared screen, in the same call where you introduce the concept — before you have "final" milestones, before you've pre-filled dates, while the buyer's own language is still available to capture instead of yours.

### Worked Example: The Live Co-Build
- **Scenario:** You've just closed a strong discovery call and want to introduce the MAP concept without it landing as "here's my sales process, please follow it."
- **Elite Script:**
  > *"Sarah, before we wrap — most deals like this fall apart not because the product is wrong, but because six different people across two companies lose track of who owes what, and by when. I want to build a shared plan with you right now, live, so nothing falls through a crack on either side. You know your internal process better than I do, so as we go, tell me if I've got a step wrong or missing. First milestone: you mentioned Legal usually takes about two weeks to turn around a standard MSA once they receive it — does that sound right, or does it run longer for a vendor like us?"*
- **Why it works:** The buyer is co-authoring their own commitments in their own language starting with the first milestone. It also gives you the same diagnostic value as the Champion test in Volume 13 — a buyer who won't engage in building the plan at all is telling you something important about how real their urgency actually is.

Once the first draft exists, review it in every subsequent call, update it the moment a date moves, and make sure every action — yours and theirs — has one accountable name attached to it, never a department.

## CHAPTER 4: RESTORING DEAL VELOCITY WHEN A MILESTONE SLIPS
When the customer misses a scheduled date on the MAP, never send a desperate follow-up email (*"Just checking in!"*). Use the MAP to restore velocity neutrally:
> *"Sarah, looking at our agreed Mutual Action Plan, we were scheduled to complete legal redlines by October 12th in order to hit your November 1st go-live date. Since legal redlines slipped by a week, that moves your rollout date to November 8th, which cuts into your lease notice deadline. Should we hop on a quick 10-minute sync with your corporate counsel today to get redlines resolved so your launch date stays protected?"*

## CHAPTER 5: COMMON MISTAKES — WHERE REPS GET THE MAP WRONG
1. **Building it alone and sending it as an attachment.** A MAP introduced via email instead of built live on a call reads as a vendor deliverable, not a shared commitment — and gets the engagement it deserves: none.
2. **Anchoring every milestone to the seller's fiscal calendar.** A plan built around "sign by end of quarter" instead of the buyer's own go-live date signals that the plan exists to help the AE, not the buyer — and buyers can tell the difference immediately.
3. **Assigning owners by department instead of by name.** "Legal" and "Procurement" cannot be held accountable; a named person with a deadline can. Every milestone needs one name attached, on both sides of the table.
4. **Overloading the plan with 30+ granular tasks.** A MAP that reads like a full implementation Gantt chart gets skimmed once and never opened again. Keep it to the handful of milestones that actually gate progress — 6 to 10, not 40.
5. **Letting the plan go stale after the kickoff call.** A MAP that isn't revisited when a date slips is worse than no MAP at all — it becomes evidence, in the buyer's mind, that the seller's own process doesn't hold up under real conditions. The moment in Chapter 4 where a milestone slips is exactly when the plan should be pulled back out, not quietly abandoned.
