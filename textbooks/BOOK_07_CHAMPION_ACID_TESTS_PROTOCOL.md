## EXECUTIVE PREFACE: THE COACH GRAVEYARD
In enterprise sales forecasting, the most common reason deals slip at the end of the quarter is that the sales rep mistook a **Friendly Coach** for a true **Champion**.

A Coach is someone who likes you, likes your software, and will gladly take a weekly call to tell you how great your demo was. But when the CFO asks *"Why do we need this?"*, the Coach shrinks into their chair, stays silent, and watches the deal die.

John McMahon — the only person to have served as CRO of five different public enterprise software companies (Geo-Tel, PTC, Ariba, BladeLogic, and BMC Software) and the author of *The Qualified Sales Leader: Proven Lessons from a Five-Time CRO* (2021) — established the definitive rule inside the MEDDPICC methodology he helped originate: **If you do not have a verified Champion, you do not have a deal.**

The stakes of getting this wrong are not theoretical. Under McMahon's leadership, Geo-Tel grew from $52 million in revenue to a $2 billion acquisition by Cisco, and BladeLogic went from a startup to an $872 million acquisition by BMC. He attributes both outcomes, in large part, to a forecasting discipline that refused to count a deal as real until a genuine Champion had been verified — not merely assumed.

## A NOTE ON THE SOURCE: MEDDIC, MEDDPICC, AND THE CHAMPION CRITERION

MEDDIC was conceived in the 1990s at Parametric Technology Corporation (PTC), where McMahon — then a sales VP — worked with colleagues Dick Dunkel and Jack Napoli to codify why PTC's best reps consistently forecasted deals accurately while everyone else guessed. The Champion letter in MEDD**I**C(C) was never about likability. It was about whether a specific internal contact had the political capital and personal motivation to move a deal forward when no salesperson was in the room — because, as the broader MEDDIC practitioner community has since quantified, the large majority of enterprise buying conversations happen without any vendor present at all.

The wider sales industry — including Force Management, which pairs MEDDIC-style qualification with its own "Command of the Message" messaging methodology — has converged on the same underlying distinction this volume teaches: **a Coach wants you to win but lacks the influence to make it happen; a Champion has that influence and is willing to spend it on your behalf.** The 3 Acid Tests in Chapter 2 exist because that distinction is invisible from a demo call — it only reveals itself under a small amount of real pressure.

## CHAPTER 2: THE 3 ACID TESTS TO TEST A CHAMPION
Never assume someone is a Champion simply because they have a VP title. You must test their allegiance with the **3 Champion Acid Tests**:

### Acid Test 1: The Proprietary Information Request
Ask for internal, confidential documentation that an outsider cannot access:
- *"Sarah, to ensure our business case aligns with your CFO's targets, can you share your current commercial lease expiration dates and your internal facilities budget allocations?"*
- **Pass:** The contact sends the documents within 48 hours or schedules a screen-share review.
- **Fail (Coach):** *"I can't share that, it's internal. Just send your standard pricing."*

### Acid Test 2: The Economic Buyer Access Test
Require access to the person who holds discretionary checkbook authority:
- *"Sarah, our implementation engineering team will need to present our EBITDA ROI model to your CFO, Dan. Can you make an introduction and join us on a 20-minute working session with him this Thursday?"*
- **Pass:** *"Let me ping Dan's executive assistant right now and get us 20 minutes."*
- **Fail (Coach):** *"Dan is very busy. Just send the proposal to me and I will present it to him myself."* (Guaranteed deal killer).

### Acid Test 3: The Internal Task Test (The Homework Test)
Assign a task that requires them to do work internally between calls:
- *"Sarah, before our next call on Tuesday, can you verify with your IT security team whether they require a custom SOC2 vendor questionnaire, or if our standard bridge letter suffices?"*
- **Pass:** They complete the task and report back with names and requirements.
- **Fail (Coach):** They show up Tuesday unprepared, saying: *"Oh, I didn't get a chance to ask them."*

## CHAPTER 3: WHERE REPS GET THIS WRONG

Even reps who know the 3 Acid Tests by heart routinely mis-qualify Champions. The failure patterns below account for the majority of "surprise" losses that show up as 90%-forecast deals that vanish in the final week.

### Mistake 1: Confusing Title With Influence
A "VP of Facilities" title tells you nothing about whether the person actually has the CFO's ear. Some Directors carry more real influence than some VPs. Test influence directly — ask who they go to when they need something approved outside their own budget — rather than inferring it from the org chart.

### Mistake 2: Declaring Victory After One Passed Test
One passed Acid Test is a data point, not a verdict. A contact who passes the Homework Test but has failed the Economic Buyer Access Test twice is still a Coach with good intentions. Champion status should be re-tested at every major deal stage, not certified once and forgotten.

### Mistake 3: Testing Too Late in the Cycle
Reps often wait until legal review or the final proposal to discover their "Champion" cannot get budget released. By then there is no time left to build a real one. Run the first Acid Test inside the first two meetings — ideally before you have invested a full demo cycle in the relationship.

### Mistake 4: Abandoning a Contact Who Fails a Test
A failed Acid Test does not mean the contact is worthless — it means they are a Coach, not a Champion. Downgrade them accordingly: keep them for intelligence gathering and internal navigation, but do not rely on them to defend your deal, and immediately multithread to find someone who can (see Volume 6: Enterprise Multithreading and MAPS).

### Mistake 5: Mistaking Friendliness for Willingness to Sell
A contact can be warm, responsive, and genuinely rooting for you and still fold the moment a skeptical peer challenges your solution in a room you are not in. Willingness to sell for you is only proven under scrutiny — which is exactly what the 3 Acid Tests are designed to simulate before the real scrutiny (procurement, legal, the executive committee) arrives.

## CHAPTER 4: GONG TRANSCRIPT: TESTING A POTENTIAL CHAMPION
*Context: AE [Your Name] speaking with Kevin, Director of Real Estate Operations at a multinational logistics firm.*

> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Kevin, your feedback on the demo has been fantastic. You mentioned that our platform can help you save $450,000 on your upcoming Dallas lease renewal. Who on your executive committee will ultimately sign off on allocating budget for this?"
>
> **Kevin (Director):** "That would be our Chief Operating Officer, Marcus. He oversees all regional real estate."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Great. Kevin, here is what typically happens in deals of this size: if you walk into Marcus's office alone with a software quote, he is going to view this as a discretionary operational tool and ask you why facilities can't just manage this in Excel. Let's do this: you and I will co-author a 1-page financial business case showing how this returns $450,000 in EBITDA. Once we finish it, let's schedule a 20-minute briefing with Marcus where you lead the meeting and I support you with the technical backup. Can you set up that session for next week?"
>
> **Kevin (Director):** "Well... Marcus is pretty intimidating. I think it's better if I just mention it to him casually at our one-on-one next month."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Kevin, let me ask you directly: if Marcus looks at your budget and decides to renew the Dallas lease as-is without any optimization, what does that mean for your space restructuring targets this year?"
>
> **Kevin (Director):** "Honestly? It ruins my performance review. I promised the executive team I would find 15% in lease savings by Q4. If I don't hit that, my annual bonus is toast."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Then your bonus is on the line. Let's make sure Marcus sees that you have already built the complete engineering and financial solution to hit that 15% target. Let's write the 1-page memo together tomorrow morning, and then we will approach Marcus as a united front. Deal?"
>
> **Kevin (Director):** "You're right. Let's do it tomorrow at 9 AM."

## CHAPTER 5: GONG TRANSCRIPT: WHEN A CONTACT FAILS THE TEST — AND HOW TO RECOVER
*Context: AE [Your Name] speaking with Priya, IT Director at a mid-market insurance carrier, three weeks into the evaluation. [Your Name] has just administered Acid Test 2.*

> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Priya, this has been a great technical evaluation. Next step is putting the business case in front of your CFO, Robert, since this is a $180,000 annual commitment. Can you get us 20 minutes with him next week?"
>
> **Priya (IT Director):** "Oh — you don't need to meet with Robert. I handle all the vendor approvals for IT. Just send me the contract and I'll get it signed."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "I appreciate that, Priya, and I know you carry a lot of weight here. Just so I understand the process correctly — does your signature alone release $180,000 in new spend, or does that require Robert's sign-off since it's outside the existing IT budget line?"
>
> **Priya (IT Director):** "It would need to go through him eventually, but I can usually just... loop him in over email once everything's finalized."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Got it — that's really useful to know. Here's my concern, and I want to be straight with you: I've seen deals like this stall for months when the Economic Buyer only sees a finished contract instead of the business case behind it. Robert is going to ask why IT needs this instead of the free tool it's replacing, and if you're the one answering that question alone, in an email thread, it puts you in a tough spot. I'd rather you not be the only one carrying that weight."
>
> **Priya (IT Director):** "That's fair. He is pretty particular about anything outside of budget."
>
> **[Your Name] (AE):** "Then let's not find that out the hard way in week eight. I'm not asking you to hand off the relationship — I'm asking you to let me support you in front of Robert so the two of you hear the ROI case together, from us, on the record. Can you check his calendar for 20 minutes this week?"
>
> **Priya (IT Director):** "Let me see what I can do. I'll email you by Friday either way."

**Analysis:** Priya's answer failed Acid Test 2 — she offered to "loop in" the Economic Buyer after the fact rather than proactively securing access, and she was vague about actual signature authority. That is a Coach pattern, not a Champion pattern. Note what [Your Name] did *not* do: bulldoze past Priya to Robert directly, which would have burned the relationship for a marginal, uncertain gain. Instead, the AE named the risk plainly, reframed the ask as protecting Priya rather than replacing her, and left a concrete, dated commitment ("email you by Friday") to hold her accountable to. If Friday passes with no update, that is itself a second failed test — and the signal to begin building a parallel path to Robert without waiting on Priya any longer.
